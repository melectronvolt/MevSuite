A lab, step by step

The principle of inertia, with the video of a puck on a table

The question: a puck slides on a table with almost no friction. Is its motion straight and uniform? We are going to measure it with MeVCine, then check it with Python. Time needed: about twenty minutes.

1. Measuring with MeVCine

  1. Start MeVCine. At the bottom right, the status bar must say “Simple mode” (otherwise: Mode › Simple mode (step by step) menu). Then 📂 Open a video… and choose the video given by your teacher (here Principe_inertie.avi: a puck on a table, with an 85 cm yellow ruler on the first frame).
  2. Origin: on the first frame, click on the left end of the yellow ruler. Next ▶
  3. Axes: choose “x to the right, y upward”. Next ▶
  4. Scale: still on the first frame (the ruler is only on it), Select the reference, click on both ends of the yellow ruler; type 85, choose cm, Confirm. Next ▶
  5. Marking: with the slider, go to frame 15: the puck is far from the hand, and its speed hardly changes any more. Click on the centre of the puck, frame after frame, looking at the magnifier, up to frame 27, the last one where it is whole (13 points). Finish marking ▶
  6. Table: check the values; redo with ⌖ a measurement that looks wrong. Next ▶
  7. Export: 📋 Copy for Python.

Before exporting, switch to expert mode (Mode › Expert mode (all features)), Trajectories tab, and tick Show the velocity vectors: arrows of the same length, in the same direction, already suggest a straight uniform motion. Then switch back to simple mode (Mode › Simple mode (step by step)): nothing is lost.

2. Working with Python

Paste the copied text (Ctrl+V) at the beginning of a program, then add:

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# speed between two successive frames, in the middle of each interval
t_middle = (lst_t[1:] + lst_t[:-1]) / 2
vx = np.diff(lst_x) / np.diff(lst_t)
vy = np.diff(lst_y) / np.diff(lst_t)
print("mean vx:", vx.mean(), "m/s")

# the straight line closest to the x(t) points
a, b = np.polyfit(lst_t, lst_x, 1)
print(f"x(t) ≈ {a:.3f} t + {b:.3f}")

plt.subplot(1, 2, 1)
plt.plot(lst_t, lst_x, "o", lst_t, a * lst_t + b, "-")
plt.xlabel("t (s)"); plt.ylabel("x (m)")
plt.subplot(1, 2, 2)
plt.plot(t_middle, vx, "o", t_middle, vy, "s")
plt.xlabel("t (s)"); plt.ylabel("speed (m/s)")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

3. Concluding

  • The y(t) points are almost constant: the trajectory is a straight line parallel to the x axis.
  • The x(t) points are aligned: x changes by the same amount in each time interval, the speed is constant. Its sign depends on the direction chosen for the x axis.
  • The speeds computed frame by frame are not all equal: small differences come from the marking (for instance, a one-pixel error on a 15-pixel displacement between two frames is almost 7 %). The more you aim at the same point of the puck, the smaller these differences.

To go further: move the origin, or change the direction of x (steps 1 and 2), and look at what changes in the table. The shape of the motion itself does not depend on the axes.
